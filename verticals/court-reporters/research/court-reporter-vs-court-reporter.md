### 1. Key Statistics and Data Points
- U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics (BLS) classifies court reporters under "Court Reporters and Simultaneous Captioners"; median annual wage $66,070 as of May 2023 (latest available); employment projected to decline 3% from 2022-2032 due to digital recording adoption, but demand persists for certified verbatim records[3].
- National Court Reporters Association (NCRA) reports ~20,000 certified stenographic court reporters in U.S.; 80% of state courts still require official court reporters for trials[1].
- Judicial Conference data: Official court reporters handle 100% of federal criminal arraignments, pleas, and sentencings without charge to court[2].
- No distinct second "court reporter" role identified; search yields single profession with subtypes (official vs. freelance/contract); 95% of depositions use freelance court reporters[1][4].

### 2. Current Pricing/Rates (2024-2026)
- Official court reporters: Paid by judicial system (federal salary ~$70,000-$120,000/year per 28 U.S.C. §753); transcripts at Judicial Conference rates: $3.65/page for ordinary, $4.85/page for expedited (2024 rates unchanged into 2026)[2].
- Freelance/deposition reporters: $3.50-$6.00/page standard transcript; $8.00-$12.00/page expedited (24-hour); $1.50-$3.00/page daily; realtime $2.50-$5.00/hour extra; half-day deposition minimum $400-$800, full-day $800-$1,500 (2024 NCRA survey averages; rates up 5-7% YoY due to shortage)[1][6].
- No dual-role pricing; single reporter covers both live capture and transcript production.

### 3. Industry Standards and Best Practices
- **Core Duties (uniform across sources)**: Verbatim capture via stenotype (95% usage), voice writing, or stenomask; produce certified transcripts; certify/filing per 28 U.S.C. §753; realtime for accessibility[1][2][3][4][7][8][9].
- **Official vs. Contract/Substitute**: Officials employed by courts for trials (front-center, impartial); contracts/freelance for depositions/hearings; both must discriminate noise/accents, exclude off-record talk, provide clarifications[1][2][6].
- **Standards**: NCRA certification (CRR for realtime, CRC for closed captioning); 225 wpm minimum speed; transcripts certified accurate within 48 hours for expedited[1][4].
- **Best Practices**: Use stenomask/machine over audio-only; read back testimony; secure exhibits; file notes 10+ years[2][7][9].
- No standard requiring "both" roles; one reporter per proceeding standard.

### 4. Common Pain Points and Solutions
- **Pain Points**:
  - Stenographer shortage: 50% drop in programs since 2000; aging workforce (avg. age 55+)[1][3].
  - Digital audio vs. stenography: Audio misses speakers/accents (90% error rate in noisy settings); no clarifications[1][6].
  - Transcript turnaround: 30-day standard too slow for appeals.
  - Cost for non-officials: $1,000+ per deposition.
- **Solutions**:
  - Realtime stenography (only proven instant voice-to-text; aids deaf access)[1].
  - Hybrid: Reporter annotates digital audio for accuracy[4].
  - Certification/automation limits: Reporters clarify ambiguities humans-only handle[1][6].
  - No "both needed"; single certified reporter resolves vs. tech alone.

### 5. Expert Opinions and Real-World Examples
- **NCRA**: "Court reporters perform functions technologies cannot: clarify inaudible speech, exclude off-record exchanges; essential for appeals/depositions"[1].
- **Judicial Conference (28 U.S.C. §753)**: Reporters must transcribe all requested proceedings promptly; categorized as official/substitute/contract—no dual requirement[2].
- **VIQ Solutions**: "Court reporters present at proceedings create verbatim record; stenographers/transcriptionists differ (latter post-process audio)"—but terms often interchangeable[4].
- **Centex Litigation**: "Automated systems fail crosstalk/legal terms; certified reporter ensures defensible record" (e.g., high-stakes trials like Enron used officials)[6].
- **Examples**: Fulton County Superior Court reporters handle jury read-backs daily; NY Courts reporters mark exhibits/read testimony in trials[5][9].

### 6. State/Regional Variations
- **Federal**: Uniform under 28 U.S.C. §753; all courts use official reporters for key criminal proceedings[2].
- **New York**: Court Reporters record/transcribe all proceedings; maintain witness logs/exhibits; realtime optional[9].
- **California/Georgia**: Freelance dominant for depositions; officials for trials (e.g., Fulton Superior: full-day coverage)[5][8].
- **Indiana (N.D. IN)**: Reporters use stenotype/stenomask; free transcripts for criminal sentencings[2].
- Variations minor: All mandate verbatim certified records; ~10 states allow audio-only for low-stakes (e.g., small claims), but 90% require reporters for trials[1].

### 7. Recent Trends or Changes (2024-2026)
- Stenographer shortage worsens: NCRA 2024 report—enrollment down 10% YoY; Project STENO scholarships up 200% to train 1,000 new reporters by 2026[1][4].
- Realtime adoption: 40% increase 2023-2025 for accessibility (ADA compliance); AI captioning rejected for legal use (90% inaccuracy in courts)[1][3].
- Digital hybrid rise: 25% proceedings use reporter-annotated audio (2024 Judicial Conference policy); full AI banned in 35 states for official records[2][6].
- Rate hikes: 6% average 2025 due to demand; federal salaries adjusted 4.5% COLA 2026[2].
- No trend for "two reporters"; consolidation to certified hybrids amid 3% job decline projection[3].
