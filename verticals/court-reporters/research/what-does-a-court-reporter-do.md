### 1. Key Statistics and Data Points
- Employment: 21,100 court reporters and simultaneous captioners employed in the U.S. in 2023, projected to decline 3% from 2023-2033 (slower than average), with 1,200 openings annually due to retirements and replacements.[2]
- Median annual wage: $66,030 in May 2024 (BLS data); top 10% earn over $113,770.[2]
- Education: 70% hold an Associate's degree; typical entry requires completion of postsecondary certificate or Associate's program (1-2 years).[2]
- Certification: Required in 50 states; national certifications include Registered Professional Reporter (RPR) from NCRA (speed test: 225 wpm).[2]
- Workforce shortage: 80% of states report court reporter shortages as of 2024, exacerbated by retirements (average age 59).[2]

### 2. Current Pricing/Rates (2026)
- Freelance deposition rates: $4.50-$7.00 per page for transcript (original + 1 copy); $3.00-$5.00 for additional copies; expedited (2-5 days): +50-100% premium; daily copy: $6.00-$10.00/page.[1][7]
- Official court reporters (government): Salaried $50,000-$90,000/year; per diem/contract: $1,200-$2,500/day for trials.[3]
- Real-time reporting: $2.50-$5.00/page extra; remote deposition: $1,500-$3,000 flat fee.[4]
- Captioning/CART: $75-$150/hour for live events.[5]

### 3. Industry Standards and Best Practices
- **Core Workflow**: Attend proceedings → Capture verbatim using stenotype/CAT (computer-aided transcription) at 225+ wpm → Administer oaths → Mark/log exhibits → Translate notes to draft transcript → Proofread/edit/certify accuracy → Deliver electronic/PDF transcripts (timestamped/indexed) within 7-30 days → Archive notes/audio.[1][2][3][4][6]
- **Equipment**: Stenotype machine (e.g., Stenograph Luminary), voice-writing (Dragon NaturallySpeaking), digital audio backup; real-time feed via CaseView software.[3][4]
- **Certifications**: NCRA RPR (minimum); CRR (real-time: 96% accuracy); CRC (captioning).[2][7]
- **Best Practices**: Maintain 99% accuracy via self-proofing; use job dictionaries for legal/medical terms; daily logs for notes/exhibits; confidentiality (HIPAA-compliant for medical); punctuality (arrive 30-60 min early).[1][3][5][6]
- **Transcript Format**: Standardized (e.g., page 25 lines, 10-pt Courier font, speaker IDs); certified with reporter signature.[5][6]

**Day-in-the-Life Example** (Official Court Reporter):
- 7:30 AM: Review calendar, setup stenotype/audio.
- 8:30 AM-4:30 PM: Report 2-3 hearings/trials; read back testimony; mark 10-50 exhibits.
- Post-session: Translate 100-200 pages notes; deliver rough draft same-day if real-time.
- Evening: Finalize transcripts (4-8 hours/page).[1][3][4]

### 4. Common Pain Points and Solutions
| Pain Point | Description | Solutions |
|------------|-------------|-----------|
| High burnout/stress | 4-8 hour sessions at 225 wpm; multitasking exhibits/oaths; deadlines (24-48 hours expedited).[2][3] | Ergonomic stenotype stands; rotation schedules; AI-assisted drafting (e.g., Stenograph Protege).[4] |
| Shortages/tech failures | 80% states short-staffed; audio backup fails 5-10% cases.[2] | Remote reporting via Zoom (post-2020 standard); hybrid human-AI transcription.[3] |
| Vocabulary gaps | Legal/medical/scientific terms (e.g., 1,000+ specialties).[1][3] | Pre-built dictionaries (20,000+ entries); brief training (e.g., "phlebotomy" = PL-B-TOE-ME).[5] |
| Physical strain | Repetitive strain injury (RSI) in 30% reporters.[2] | Voice-writing shift (20% adoption); shorter sessions (max 4 hours).[4][8] |
| Confidentiality breaches | Handling sensitive cases (e.g., criminal trials).[7] | Encrypted file transfer; secure cloud archival (e.g., Chief Official Reporter oversight).[3] |

- **Official vs. Freelance**: NY/CA: 70% official (salaried, unionized); South (e.g., TX/FL): 60% freelance/contract.[6]
- **Pay Scales**: Highest in CA ($92,500 median), DC ($110,000); lowest MS ($45,000).[2]
